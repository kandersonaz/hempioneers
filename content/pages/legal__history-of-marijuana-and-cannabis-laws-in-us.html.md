<p><em><strong>Pre-Independence Days</strong></em></p>
<p>When the Unites States was a young colony, England demanded payment of taxes by requiring some colonies to grow and export hemp. One could argue factually the first formal tax system in the colonies which became the United States of America required payment in marijuana.</p>
<p>Regulation of <i><a title="Cannabis sativa" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_sativa">Cannabis sativa</a></i> began as early as 1619. The Virginia Company, by decree of <a class="mw-redirect" title="King James I" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_I">King James I</a> in 1619, ordered every colonist to grow 100 plants specifically for export. Thus, England&#8217;s only colony in America began to grow<a title="Hemp" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemp">hemp</a> in order to meet this obligation and, soon, to serve a growing demand in other colonies.<sup id="cite_ref-2" class="reference"><a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_history_of_cannabis_in_the_United_States#cite_note-2">[2]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-a_breif_chronology_of_Marijuana_in_America_3-0" class="reference"><a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_history_of_cannabis_in_the_United_States#cite_note-a_breif_chronology_of_Marijuana_in_America-3">[3]</a></sup> <a title="George Washington" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington">George Washington</a> grew hemp at <a title="Mount Vernon" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Vernon">Mount Vernon</a> as one of his three primary crops. The use of hemp for rope and fabric was ubiquitous throughout the 18th and 19th centuries in the United States. Medicinal preparations of cannabis became available in American <a class="mw-redirect" title="Pharmacies" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmacies">pharmacies</a> in the 1850s following an introduction to its use in Western medicine by <a title="William Brooke O'Shaughnessy" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Brooke_O%27Shaughnessy">William O&#8217;Shaughnessy</a> a decade earlier in 1839.<sup id="cite_ref-Dale_H._Gieringer_4-0" class="reference"><a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_history_of_cannabis_in_the_United_States#cite_note-Dale_H._Gieringer-4">[4]</a></sup></p>

<p><em><strong>The Puritanicallt Charged Unenlightened Days of the Early 1900s</strong></em></p>
<p>Around the same time, efforts to regulate the sale of <a class="mw-redirect" title="Pharmaceuticals" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmaceuticals">pharmaceuticals</a> began, and laws were introduced on a state-to-state basis that created penalties for mislabeling drugs, <a title="Adulterant" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adulterant">adulterating</a> them with undisclosed <a class="mw-redirect" title="Narcotics" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narcotics">narcotics</a>, and improper sale of those considered &#8220;<a title="Poison" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poison">poisons</a>&#8220;. Poison laws generally either required labels on the packaging indicating the harmful effects of the drugs or prohibited sale outside of licensed pharmacies and without a doctor&#8217;s prescription. Those that required labeling often required the word &#8220;poison&#8221; if the drug was not issued by a pharmacy. Other regulations were prohibitions on the sale to minors, as well as restrictions on refills. Some pharmaceutical laws specifically enumerated the drugs that came under the effect of the regulations, while others didn&#8217;t — leaving the matter to medical experts. Those that did generally included references to cannabis, either under the category of &#8220;cannabis and its preparations&#8221; or &#8220;hemp and its preparations.&#8221;<sup id="cite_ref-5" class="reference"><a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_history_of_cannabis_in_the_United_States#cite_note-5">[5]</a></sup></p>
<p>Increased restrictions and labeling of cannabis as a poison began in many states from 1906 onward, and outright prohibitions began in the 1920s. By the mid-1930s Cannabis was regulated as a drug in every state, including 35 states that adopted the <a title="Uniform State Narcotic Drug Act" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_State_Narcotic_Drug_Act">Uniform State Narcotic Drug Act</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-druglibrary.org_1-0" class="reference"><a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_history_of_cannabis_in_the_United_States#cite_note-druglibrary.org-1">[1]</a></sup></p>
<p><em><strong>Marihuana Tax Act of 1937</strong></em></p>
<p>Prior to the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937, marijuana was widely used by medical doctors in the United States for a variety of physical and emotional ailments. The full text of the act can be <a href="http://www.druglibrary.org/schaffer/hemp/taxact/mjtaxact.htm" target="_blank">read here</a>. Extensive background on the act been be read <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marihuana_Tax_Act_of_1937" target="_blank">here</a>. It was not apparent in public debate at that time that the purpose of the act was to make marijauna illegal in the US, but it had that effect due to the nature of its enforcement provisions.</p>
<p>In the 1970s, many places in the United States started to abolish state laws and other local regulations that banned possession or sale of cannabis. The same thing happened with cannabis sold as <a title="Medical cannabis" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medical_cannabis">medical cannabis</a> in the 1990s.</p>
<p>All this is in conflict with federal laws; cannabis is a <a title="List of Schedule I drugs (US)" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Schedule_I_drugs_(US)">Schedule I</a> drug according to the <a title="Controlled Substances Act" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_Substances_Act">Controlled Substances Act</a> of 1970, which classified cannabis as having high potential for abuse, no medical use, and not safe to use without medical supervision. Multiple efforts to <a title="Removal of cannabis from Schedule I of the Controlled Substances Act" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Removal_of_cannabis_from_Schedule_I_of_the_Controlled_Substances_Act">reschedule cannabis</a> under the Act have failed, and the <a class="mw-redirect" title="United States Supreme Court" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Supreme_Court">United States Supreme Court</a> has ruled in <i><a title="United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers' Cooperative" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_v._Oakland_Cannabis_Buyers%27_Cooperative">United States v. Oakland Cannabis Buyers&#8217; Cooperative</a></i> and <i><a title="Gonzales v. Raich" href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonzales_v._Raich">Gonzales v.
